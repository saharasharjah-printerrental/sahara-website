# Designing Employee ID Cards That Survive Daily Use (and the Printers That Make Them)

A staff badge is the most-seen piece of brand design your client will ever produce. It sits at eye level in every meeting, drifts through the background of a thousand photos, and gets scanned at a dozen doors a day — and yet in most companies it's designed in an afternoon, after the printer has already been bought, by whoever has a template login.

For agencies working with corporate clients in the UAE, the employee badge is a rare opportunity: brand work with a genuinely functional brief. But it comes with constraints most designers never meet until the first batch prints — and those constraints start not with the artwork but with the printer the artwork is destined for. Here's what actually matters, from canvas to card material to the credentialing rush of exhibition season.

## Start with the physical object

The standard badge is CR-80: 85.60 × 53.98 mm — the same footprint as a bank card — and usually 0.76 mm thick. Design at exactly those dimensions at 300 DPI minimum, and build in the realities of the object:

**The lanyard hole is coming for your layout.** Most badges are punched with a slot near a short edge or a round hole for a clip. Anything within roughly 5 mm of that zone — a logo, the top of a photo, a letter of someone's name — will be destroyed. Agree the slot position with the client before placing a single element, and keep it consistent across the workforce: badges punched in different places by different suppliers look sloppy on a wall of them.

**Orientation is a door-reader decision.** Vertical badges read better on a lanyard; horizontal badges fit more text and a larger photo. Check how the client's door readers are mounted — some reader designs make one orientation awkward to tap — and how security staff glance-check badges at a distance.

**Photo placement is hierarchy.** The photo is the most-scanned element on the card, by machines and humans alike. It deserves the largest single block of visual weight, shot under consistent lighting (a fixed booth setup beats a phone camera and a corridor wall), and positioned where a lanyard or clip won't cover half of it.

## Bleed decides which printer you can use

Here's the constraint almost no design brief mentions: not every card printer can print edge to edge.

A direct-to-card printer prints straight onto the card's surface and leaves a thin white border — usually under a millimetre — around the image. A retransfer printer prints onto a clear film and bonds the film to the card, so the image goes truly over the edge, borderless.

Design consequence: if the identity uses a full-bleed dark background or edge-to-edge photography, a direct-to-card machine forces you to accept a white frame (fine on some layouts, ruinous on others) or step up to retransfer hardware. If the design carries a deliberate white margin, direct-to-card is invisible and you save the client money. Smart agencies flip the sequence: spec the card design to the hardware actually in place — or, during a rebrand where badge printing is in scope, make the bleed decision part of the procurement conversation rather than a surprise at print time.

## Colour on plastic is not colour on screen

Dye-sublimation card printers build colour in CMYK from a ribbon — not the RGB of your artboard, and not the Pantone of the brand book. Notes from hard experience:

- **Brand blues and greys drift.** Deep navy often prints darker and duller; light greys can band. Proof on actual cards, under actual office lighting, before sign-off. One physical test card saves a reprint of five hundred.
- **Fine type needs restraint.** Light-weight type below about 6 pt fills in, and small reversed-out text on dark backgrounds suffers most. Keep critical details — name, ID number — in clean, medium-weight type.
- **Matte beats gloss for usability.** Gloss overlay looks premium in a portfolio shot and turns into a mirror under fluorescent corridor lighting and phone-camera flash. A matte or satin finish keeps the photo legible at a glance — and a badge that can't be read at a glance fails its one job.
- **Metallic is a ribbon, not an ink.** The RTAI's HOLO-MET metallic ribbons print real silver and gold accents — a client-dazzling detail for VIP or membership tiers that is also impossible to fake convincingly on a desktop printer, which is rather the point.

## Design for machines as much as people

Modern badges carry data: QR codes for visitor management, barcodes for canteen tills, RFID for door access. Each has layout rules that get broken constantly:

- **QR codes need quiet zones.** Keep a clear margin around any code; don't butt it against the card edge or the photo. Test-scan with the client's actual reader app, from the distance staff will really hold it — often a worn badge dangling at waist height.
- **Contrast survives scanners; decoration doesn't.** A QR code knocked back to 40% tint so it "blends with the design" is a code that stops scanning. Treat functional marks as functional.
- **Names are multicultural here.** A UAE workforce badge may carry names in two languages and several naming conventions. Agree the hierarchy with HR: which form of the name goes large (the one colleagues actually use), how long names truncate, and whether Arabic and English run side by side or front and back. Nothing dates a badge system faster than a truncation rule nobody planned.

## Material is part of the design

"Plastic card" covers a wider range than clients expect. Standard PVC suits a one-year employee badge. Composite and PET cards resist heat — relevant when half your client's staff leave badges in parked cars through an August afternoon. Rewritable cards suit visitor passes reissued daily. And for boutique hospitality or wellness clients, the RTAI's support for specially treated wooden and translucent cards is a genuinely distinctive option: a membership card that feels like an object, not a hotel key.

Durability finishes the story. Retransfer printing seals the image under its film, while direct-to-card cards need an overlaminate module for equivalent scratch resistance. For anything worn daily on a lanyard — rubbing against door frames, keys, and badge clips — specify the protected route, or the design you worked on will look tired by month six.

## The exhibition-season special

Agencies get pulled into credentialing constantly: a client's three-day conference at a Dubai exhibition centre, a product launch with tiered access, a festival where crew, artists, VIPs, and press need visually different badges, fast. Two workable models:

**Rent a card printer for the event.** A desktop card printer, a laptop, ribbon stock, and blank cards turn badge production into an on-site station — a lost badge reprints in minutes, not courier days. Ask the rental provider what's included: ribbons and blanks, encoding modules if badges double as access credentials, spare hardware on call, and delivery to the venue. UAE rental plans for this class of printer start from around AED 250/month, so a week-long event costs less than the stand's coffee budget.

**Brief a bureau.** Send the attendee list and tier artwork; receive finished, lanyard-punched badges. Less control on the day, zero hardware — the right call when the design is locked weeks in advance.

Either way, treat badges as part of the event identity, not an operations afterthought: colour-coded tiers, type sized to be read across a crowded registration desk, and a design that photographs well — because a hundred attendees will have it on camera by lunch.

## The takeaway

A badge succeeds when three things align: a layout that respects the card's physical realities, a print process matched to the design's ambitions, and materials chosen for the life the badge will actually live. Designers who understand all three stop being handed the artwork task at the end of a rebrand — they get consulted at the start, where the printer, the materials, and the identity are decided together.
